      Decision records

      One decision, two documents: an ADR and a memo

      Lumen Supply picked a model tier and a retrieval design in a chat thread, and six months later nobody can say why. A new team lead asks for the reasons, the alternatives and the way back, and the answer is a search through messages. This lesson writes the decision down twice, once for the engineers and once for the sponsor. This is the support desk at Lumen Supply, an online electronics shop. You will read a decision thread and see a checker accept a memo with no cost figure and no time to undo the change. Then you write the ADR and a checker that scores each section.

      I doWatch the failure and its fix.

      A decision nobody can explain

      A decision record is read by someone who was not in the room. An architecture decision record, an ADR, holds the context, the options, the decision, the consequences and the rollback in one place.

      A memo is the one page a sponsor reads before approving the spend. The model drafts the memo, the checker scores it, and the team owns the decision.

      Three things go wrong. The decision lives in chat, so its reasons and alternatives are lost. One document serves the sponsor and the engineers, so it suits neither. And no implementation guide or rollback plan sits beside the decision.

      Two documents, one checker

      check_memo returns the sections that are missing or too weak: the outcome, the cost, the ask, the risk and the rollback. Each part is found by the words in its heading, so "How to Undo" and "Rollback" get the same verdict. A refused draft goes back to the model with the sections to fix.

      A section is weak when it is a few words or says nothing was recorded. The cost needs a money figure, and the rollback needs a time and a trigger. The ADR carries both, so the model has something to say.

      The arithmetic is plain. Rebuilding a lost reason takes a week of asking around, and writing it down takes an hour.

      The implementation guide

      The ADR has a guide that lists the steps in order. Each step names an owner, and a check says whether the change did what the ADR promised. A guide with no owner has nobody to call when a step fails. Enforce in managed settings and hooks ships it to the team.

      The ADR also has a life after launch. It names a review date, 12 March, and an owner, Priya, who compares the measured outcome with the promise. A record that is never reviewed cannot be corrected when the numbers drift.

      A reversed decision is never deleted. The new ADR marks the old one superseded and links back, so a later reader sees what was tried. The lifecycle plan also covers the end: retiring the agent names an owner, a date and what happens to the stored tickets.
